# Product-led design decisions

Use this for a substantial new interface or creative redesign. It strengthens
the decision process; it does not change the model's reasoning capability or
promise a particular price, award, conversion rate, or agency valuation.

## Understand the use case

Inspect the project before asking questions. Establish the audience, primary
visitor task, product truth, content/assets, existing brand, platform, scope,
and delivery constraints. Distinguish a page that persuades from a screen people
operate or read. Record unknown facts as assumptions, not invented capabilities.

For each important component, state what the visitor needs to decide, compare,
understand, or do. Derive its anatomy and states from that task. For example,
a configurable object needs linked shape/material/price feedback; a professional
service needs inspectable evidence of expertise; an app needs clear working
states and recovery. None automatically needs a carousel, 3D scene, or card grid.

## Ask one useful discovery round

For an open substantial brief, ask one to three focused questions after inspection
when meaningful choices remain. Reuse answers already present in the conversation
or project. Do not ask users to select skills, technical packages, exact hex values,
or dozens of fonts. Do not interrupt narrow fixes with a brand questionnaire.

Choose the missing questions that materially affect the result:

- What should the primary visitor accomplish, and who is that visitor?
- Which character fits the brand: quiet and precise, editorial and crafted,
  or expressive and immersive? Recommend the option supported by the product,
  explain each option in one sentence, and accept another direction.
- Which references, brand rules, assets, launch constraints, or real budget must
  the design respect? Ask for budget only if it changes feasible work, such as
  photography, paid assets, or a live integration; never imply approval to spend.

Use the host's question tool when available; otherwise ask concise full sentences.
Continue independent inspection and reference research while answers are pending.
Give optional questions a reasonable opportunity for a reply, normally at least
60 seconds; then state an assumption if progress can safely continue. Required
answers, such as an unresolved brand choice the user insists on making, remain
pending. Time passing is never approval. If the user delegates choices or says
not to ask, proceed and briefly explain the direction. Do not repeat settled
questions or create a second routine approval gate before implementation.

## Compare directions, not adjectives

Consider two or three plausible directions, then make a concise decision record:

| Direction | Product fit | Page/component structure | Typography and palette | Assets/motion | Tradeoff |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Project-specific name | Evidence from audience/task | Distinct information journey and focal mechanism | Exact candidates and semantic roles | Available resources and useful behavior | Readability, cost, implementation or performance risk |

These are candidates, not three full implementations. If the user wants visual
options, render representative focal compositions using Prototype or the available
image workflow; do not present textual labels as tested visual alternatives.
Keep the record to observable decisions and short rationales, not a transcript
of internal deliberation. Choose against product fit, comprehension, distinctness,
asset feasibility, accessibility, and implementation budget. Avoid fake precision
such as unsupported numerical taste scores.

Minimal, premium, and expressive are not quality tiers. A minimal interface can
be the most carefully crafted choice. An editorial direction can use bold color;
an expensive-looking page need not use gold, black, tiny text, or a serif. Treat
"$1,000+", "luxury", or "agency quality" as a request for craft and completeness,
then clarify the desired character rather than claiming market value.

## Choose component style and color deliberately

Define the signature component's content model, anatomy, hierarchy, states,
input methods, responsive composition, and motion ownership before catalog
shopping. Compare mechanisms that answer the same visitor question. A borrowed
hover link is useful polish, not evidence of a major original component.

For an unfixed brand, compare two plausible palette families in representative
content, including the actual primary action, body text, focus, and status states.
Explain why the chosen family suits the product and audience without relying on
industry stereotypes. Preserve established brand colors when the scope requires it.
Specify exact color roles and contrast; do not choose from palette names alone.

Choose font roles from reading density, product character, language/glyph support,
numeric content, and licensed availability. Test real headings, long controls,
prices, paragraphs, and fallback wrapping. Refine spacing, weight, measure, and
mobile line breaks in the render. Defaulting to the last project's font/color
combination is not a design decision.

Use one selected direction throughout. Revisit it only when new product facts,
user feedback, or a rendered failure warrants a change.
